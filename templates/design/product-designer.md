# Product Designer Resume Template

*For designers who own end-to-end product experiences — from problem framing through shipped UI.*

Product design roles are evaluated on both craft and judgment: can you turn an ambiguous problem into a well-reasoned, well-executed interface, and can you explain the tradeoffs you made along the way? Hiring teams are also checking whether you can operate across the full lifecycle — research, structure, visual execution, and measurement — rather than just one slice of it. This template gives you a structure that surfaces all of that at once.

## Resume Structure

1. **Header** — name, contact, portfolio link (a strong portfolio matters more here than in almost any other role).
2. **Summary** — 2-3 lines on your design focus (e.g. 0-to-1 product design, growth, platform/design systems).
3. **Skills** — tools, methods, and collaboration surface area.
4. **Experience** — reverse-chronological, framed around problems solved and outcomes, not just deliverables.
5. **Selected Projects** (optional if experience is thin) — 1-2 case studies with a link to the full write-up.
6. **Education**.

## Key Competency Areas

- Translating ambiguous problems into clear, well-reasoned design directions
- End-to-end process: research, flows, wireframes, high-fidelity UI, prototyping
- Balancing user needs, business goals, and technical constraints within one design
- Collaboration with PM and engineering through the entire build cycle, not just handoff
- Using data and user feedback to validate or revise design decisions
- Systems thinking — consistency, reusability, and design system contribution
- Communicating design rationale clearly to non-designers and leadership
- Owning outcomes for a product area, not just individual deliverables
- Prioritizing across competing constraints when timelines, resourcing, or scope shift mid-project
- Advocating for the user in tradeoff conversations with product and engineering
- Knowing when to go deep on craft versus move fast toward a shippable outcome

## Bullet Point Framework

Structure each bullet as **action + scope + measurable impact**: what you did, how big the surface area was (users, team, product area), and what changed as a result. A bullet that only names a deliverable ("designed a checkout flow") tells a reviewer nothing about judgment or effect — the scope and outcome are what separate a strong bullet from a task list.

- Redesigned the [product area] onboarding flow, reducing drop-off at [step] by [X%] across [N] users
- Led end-to-end design for [feature], from problem framing through launch, shipped in [timeframe]
- Partnered with [PM/eng] to scope and prototype [feature], validating direction with [N] user interviews before build
- Introduced [pattern/component] to the design system, reused across [N] surfaces and reducing design debt in [area]
- Ran a usability study on [flow], identifying [finding] that informed a redesign improving [metric] by [X%]
- Owned design for [product area] end-to-end, contributing to a [X%] increase in [key business metric]
- Mentored [N] junior designers on [skill area], raising the quality bar for [team/product line]
- Facilitated design reviews with [PM/eng/leadership], aligning [N] stakeholders on direction before build

## Tips for Product Designer Resumes

- Your portfolio does the heavy lifting — the resume's job is to get someone to open it. Make the link impossible to miss.
- Lead bullets with the problem or decision, not the tool. "Redesigned checkout to reduce cart abandonment" beats "Designed screens in Figma."
- Quantify outcomes wherever you can, even directional ones ("reduced support tickets related to X").
- If you worked on a team, be specific about your role versus the team's — reviewers want to know what *you* owned.
- Show range across the process, not just final screens — mention research or validation somewhere, even briefly.
- Cut visual-only descriptions ("clean, modern UI") — they don't differentiate and don't belong on a resume.
- If you've led or mentored other designers, say so explicitly — leadership signals matter even in individual-contributor searches.
