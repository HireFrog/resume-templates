# UX Researcher Resume Template

*For researchers who generate insight that shapes product and design decisions.*

UX research resumes are judged on rigor and influence: did your research use sound methods, and did it actually change what got built? This is a different bar than visual craft — reviewers are looking at your choice of method, the quality of your synthesis, and evidence that findings moved a real decision rather than sitting in a report. This template centers on methods and downstream impact rather than visual output.

## Resume Structure

1. **Header** — name, contact, portfolio or writing samples link (case studies or research reports if available).
2. **Summary** — your research focus (e.g. generative research, usability testing, mixed-methods).
3. **Skills** — research methods, analysis tools, domains.
4. **Experience** — reverse-chronological, framed around studies run and decisions influenced.
5. **Selected Studies** — 1-2 research case studies with links, if available.
6. **Education** — degree and institution; note a research-adjacent field (psychology, HCI, sociology) here if relevant.
7. **Publications/Talks** *(optional)* — conference talks or published research, if they exist.

## Key Competency Areas

- Choosing the right method for the question (generative vs. evaluative, qual vs. quant)
- Study design: recruiting, protocols, moderation
- Synthesis: turning raw findings into clear, actionable insight
- Communicating findings to influence product and design decisions
- Partnering with design and product throughout the process, not just at the end
- Statistical or survey literacy where quantitative methods are part of the toolkit
- Building a repeatable research practice (repositories, playbooks) where relevant
- Navigating ambiguity — scoping a study when the question itself isn't fully formed
- Maintaining research rigor and participant ethics (consent, bias awareness, sampling)
- Translating stakeholder questions into answerable, well-scoped research questions

## Bullet Point Framework

Structure each bullet as **method + what you investigated + decision or outcome it changed**. Name the method precisely rather than saying "did research," and always close the loop on what happened because of the finding — a study that didn't change a decision is much weaker evidence than one that did, even if the study itself was well executed.

- Ran [N] moderated usability sessions on [flow], surfacing [finding] that led to a redesign reducing drop-off by [X%]
- Led generative research into [problem space], synthesizing [N] interviews into [N] key themes that shaped the [quarter] roadmap
- Designed and ran a mixed-methods study (survey + interviews) on [topic], informing the decision to [outcome]
- Built a research repository of [N] past studies, cutting duplicate research effort by [X%]
- Partnered with [PM/design] to validate [concept] pre-build, preventing investment in a direction users rejected
- Analyzed survey data from [N] respondents, identifying [segment/pattern] that reprioritized [initiative]
- Facilitated a research readout with [stakeholders], turning [finding] into [N] concrete design or roadmap changes

## Tips for UX Researcher Resumes

- Lead every bullet with the decision your research changed, not just the study you ran.
- Name your methods precisely (moderated usability test, diary study, survey) — vague terms like "did research" undersell rigor.
- If you can't share confidential findings, describe the method and impact in general terms rather than omitting the work.
- Show range across the research spectrum (generative and evaluative) if you have it — it signals versatility.
- Mention any repeatable practice you built (repository, playbook, panel) — it shows you scale impact beyond one study at a time.
- Include sample sizes and participant counts where you can; they signal rigor even without a business metric.
- If you've presented findings directly to leadership or cross-functional stakeholders, say so — it shows research influence, not just execution.
