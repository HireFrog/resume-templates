# Engineering Manager Resume Template

*For engineers who now manage a team and are hired to deliver through people, not just code.*

An Engineering Manager resume needs to prove two things at once: that you understand the technical work well enough to make good calls, and that you can grow, direct, and retain a team that ships. Hiring managers are scanning for team-level outcomes — delivery, quality, retention — not individual commits. The strongest resumes at this level read as a track record of people decisions and delivery outcomes, backed by just enough technical detail to establish credibility.

## Resume Structure

1. **Header** — name, location, email, phone, LinkedIn, GitHub (optional at this level).
2. **Summary** — 2-3 lines: team size managed, domain, and a headline outcome.
3. **Core Competencies** — a short line of skills (people management, technical strategy, roadmap planning, hiring).
4. **Experience** — reverse-chronological, with team scope and delivery outcomes per role.
5. **Technical Background** — brief note on prior IC experience and current technical fluency.
6. **Certifications & Training** (optional) — management or technical certifications if relevant to the target role.
7. **Education** — degree and institution.

## Key Competency Areas

- Hiring, onboarding, and retaining engineers
- Performance management, coaching, and career growth conversations
- Translating business goals into a team roadmap
- Balancing technical debt against feature delivery
- Cross-functional partnership with product and design
- Incident response and on-call ownership at the team level
- Team health: velocity, morale, and attrition
- Running agile ceremonies (planning, retros, stand-ups) that the team actually finds useful
- Making and defending prioritization calls when engineering, product, and design disagree
- Onboarding new hires quickly enough that they contribute meaningfully within their first quarter

## Bullet Point Framework

Use **Action + Scope + Measurable Impact**: lead with the verb that describes what you did, name the size or shape of the team or system it touched, and close with the outcome in numbers. Avoid bullets that describe responsibilities without a result — "managed a team of engineers" tells a reader what your job title meant, not whether you were good at it. Every bullet should answer "and what happened because of this."

- Grew and managed a team of [N] engineers, reducing regrettable attrition from [X%] to [Y%] over [timeframe]
- Restructured the on-call rotation across [N] engineers, cutting after-hours pages by [X%]
- Led the team through a [migration/re-architecture], shipping on time with [X%] fewer production incidents
- Hired [N] engineers in [timeframe], reducing average time-to-fill from [X] to [Y] weeks
- Partnered with product to reprioritize the roadmap, increasing on-time delivery from [X%] to [Y%]
- Introduced [process, e.g. sprint planning changes], improving team velocity by [X%]

## Tips for Engineering Manager Resumes

- Lead with team outcomes, not personal code contributions — the reader wants to know what your team shipped under your leadership, not what you personally wrote.
- Quantify team size, scope, and delivery metrics wherever possible; vague claims like "led a high-performing team" don't differentiate you from every other applicant.
- Keep a visible thread of technical credibility — a line on your IC background or current technical involvement matters, especially for smaller companies where managers still ship code.
- Don't hide people-management wins behind engineering jargon; hiring, coaching, and retention are core deliverables at this level and deserve their own bullets, not a footnote.
- If you managed through a difficult period (layoffs, reorg, incident-heavy quarter), frame what you stabilized rather than only the disruption itself.
- Show growth in the team you inherited versus the one you left — improvement over time is one of the clearest signals of good management, even more than the end state alone.
- Name the products or systems your team owned, even briefly; it gives non-technical and technical readers alike a concrete anchor for the scope of your role.
- If you're moving from a larger to a smaller company (or vice versa), acknowledge the resourcing difference directly rather than letting the reader guess why your metrics look different.
