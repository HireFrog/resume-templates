# Business Intelligence Analyst Resume Template

*For analysts who build the dashboards, reports, and metrics that leadership relies on to run the business.*

Business Intelligence Analyst roles are judged less on one-off analysis and more on the reliability, adoption, and clarity of the reporting infrastructure you own. Hiring managers want to know: do people actually use your dashboards, do executives trust the numbers, and can you gather requirements from a stakeholder who doesn't fully know what they're asking for yet. This template is built around that reporting-and-trust throughline, distinct from the deeper statistical framing of a data scientist's resume.

## Resume Structure

1. **Header** — name, location, phone, email, LinkedIn/portfolio.
2. **Summary** — 2-3 lines on the business domain you report on and the audience your dashboards serve (executives, ops, sales).
3. **Skills** — BI platforms, data querying, and reporting/stakeholder management skills, grouped by category.
4. **Professional Experience** — reverse-chronological, weighted toward dashboard adoption and decisions your reporting enabled.
5. **Projects** (optional) — sample dashboards or reporting builds, especially useful for demonstrating design sense.
6. **Education** — degree, institution, year.

## Key Competency Areas

- Building and maintaining dashboards in enterprise BI tools (Tableau, Power BI, Looker, or similar)
- SQL proficiency for pulling, joining, and shaping data from warehouse or transactional systems
- Requirements gathering — translating a stakeholder's vague ask into a defined, buildable report
- Defining and standardizing KPIs so metrics mean the same thing across teams
- Executive and stakeholder reporting — presenting data clearly to audiences with limited time and technical background
- Data governance and accuracy — ensuring dashboards stay correct as underlying data changes
- Dashboard design and UX — building reports people actually open and use, not just ones that exist
- Managing multiple stakeholder requests and reporting priorities simultaneously

## Bullet Point Framework

Use **Action + Reporting Artifact/Scope + Adoption or Decision Impact**: describe the dashboard or report you built, who it served, and evidence it was actually used or changed a decision. A report nobody opens is not an accomplishment — the differentiator is adoption and impact.

- Built an [executive dashboard] tracking [revenue and retention metrics] across [5 business units], adopted as the weekly leadership review standard
- Standardized [X] conflicting definitions of [a core KPI] into one governed metric, eliminating recurring "which number is right" disputes
- Reduced report request turnaround from [X days] to [same-day] by building a [self-service Looker/Power BI model] for [sales/ops team]
- Gathered requirements from [C-level stakeholder] and delivered a [dashboard] within [X weeks], now used in [monthly board reporting]
- Migrated [legacy reporting] from [static spreadsheets] to [Tableau/Power BI], saving [X hours/week] of manual report generation
- Improved dashboard adoption from [X to Y users/week] by [redesigning UX / adding drill-down capability / stakeholder training]

## Tips for Business Intelligence Analyst Resumes

- Lead with adoption and decision impact, not just what the dashboard shows — "used in weekly leadership review" beats "built a dashboard tracking revenue."
- Name your BI platform explicitly (Tableau, Power BI, Looker) — many postings filter on tool experience, and fluency in one doesn't always transfer credit for another on a resume screen.
- Include at least one bullet about resolving conflicting metric definitions; it's one of the clearest signals of BI maturity beyond just report-building.
- Mention the seniority of your audience (executives, VPs, board) where true — it signals your comfort presenting under scrutiny.
- Show both technical depth (SQL, data modeling) and design sense (dashboard usability, adoption) — the strongest BI resumes demonstrate both.
- Keep the resume to one page for most experience levels; let the work speak through outcomes, not tool lists.
