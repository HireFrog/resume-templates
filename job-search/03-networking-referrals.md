# Networking & Referrals

> **Format:** Markdown (`.md`) · **Designed for:** Humans + AI agents

*For anyone who'd rather send a hundred cold applications than one networking message — why referrals matter, and how to reach out without it feeling transactional.*

A referral typically moves a resume from a pile of hundreds to a direct hand-off to a hiring manager, which is why networking is one of the highest-leverage things you can do in a search — and also one of the most avoided, usually because it feels transactional or uncomfortable. This guide covers how to network in a way that doesn't feel like that, and how to actually ask for a referral once you have a relationship.

## Warm Outreach

The easiest networking is reconnecting with people you already know, not cold messaging strangers. Before reaching out to anyone new, go through:

- Former colleagues and managers
- Classmates and alumni
- People from past teams, projects, or clients
- Anyone you've had a genuine professional interaction with, even briefly

When you reach out, lead with the relationship, not the ask. A short, specific message outperforms a long one:

> "Hey [Name] — it's been a while! I saw you're still at [Company] and doing great things with [specific thing, if you know it]. I'm currently exploring [type of role] and wanted to reconnect. Would you have 15 minutes sometime in the next couple weeks to catch up?"

Notice this doesn't ask for a job or a referral in the first message. Reconnect first, ask second — a request that arrives after real conversation lands very differently than one that arrives cold.

If you're reaching out to someone you don't already know — a second-degree connection, an alum you've never met, someone in a target role at a target company — be upfront and specific about why you're reaching out and keep the ask small:

> "Hi [Name], I'm [your name], a fellow [school/program] alum. I'm exploring a move into [field/role] and came across your background at [Company] — I'd love to hear how you got into the work and what it's actually like day to day, if you're open to a short call."

## Informational Interviews

An informational interview is a short, low-pressure conversation where you're asking someone about their role, team, or company — not asking for a job. Done well, it's useful for both sides: you get real information you can't get from a job posting, and the other person gets a low-stakes, positive interaction with you that they'll remember later.

A few guidelines:

- **Keep it to 15-20 minutes** and respect that time exactly, even if the conversation is going well.
- **Come with specific questions**, not "tell me about your job." Ask about what changed in the role over time, what makes someone succeed on the team, what they wish they'd known going in.
- **Don't ask for a job in the meeting.** If a referral or an opening comes up naturally, let the other person raise it. If it doesn't, that's fine — the relationship itself was the point.
- **Follow up with a genuine thank-you** referencing something specific from the conversation, not a generic template.

## Alumni Networks

School and program alumni networks are underused because they feel formal, but alumni tend to respond to outreach from fellow alumni at a meaningfully higher rate than cold contacts do — there's a built-in reason to say yes. Check for:

- A formal alumni directory or platform through your school's career office
- Alumni-specific groups on professional networks
- Alumni panels, mixers, or mentorship programs, even virtual ones

The same warm-outreach approach applies: lead with the shared connection, ask for a short conversation, and don't rush to the ask.

## Asking for a Referral Without It Being Awkward

The discomfort around asking for a referral usually comes from asking too early or too bluntly. A few things that make it feel natural instead:

- **Earn it first.** A referral request lands best after there's already some relationship — a past working history, or at least one real conversation (an informational interview counts).
- **Make it easy to say yes.** Don't just ask "can you refer me" — send your resume, the specific job link, and a short blurb about why you're a fit that they could forward with minimal effort on their part.
- **Give them an easy out.** Something like: "No worries at all if it's not a fit for a referral — just wanted to flag it in case you had bandwidth." This removes the pressure and, ironically, tends to increase the odds they'll help.
- **Say thank you regardless of outcome**, and follow up to let them know how it went. People remember who kept them in the loop, which matters if you ever want to ask again.

Example message once you know someone and see an opening at their company:

> "Hey [Name], hope you're doing well! I saw [Company] has an opening for [role] and it looks like a strong fit for what I'm doing next. Would you be open to referring me, or connecting me with the hiring manager? Happy to send over my resume and a quick note on why I'm interested — and totally understand if it's not the right moment to ask."

## Keeping the Network Warm

Networking that only flows in one direction runs dry fast, and a network you only contact when you need something feels exactly like that to the people in it. A few habits keep it healthier over the long run:

- Look for small ways to be useful without being asked — sharing a relevant article, making an introduction of your own, congratulating people on moves and launches.
- Reconnect periodically even when you don't need anything, so the first message someone gets from you in two years isn't a job ask.
- Once you land somewhere, stay in touch with the people who helped along the way. This is what turns a one-time favor into a durable relationship instead of a single transaction.
- Keep a lightweight, informal note of who you know where — even a short list of names, companies, and "last talked" dates makes it far easier to reconnect naturally instead of losing track of a network you spent real time building.
