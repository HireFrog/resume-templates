# Finding Jobs

*For anyone whose search is limited to scrolling one big aggregator — a map of where real openings actually come from, and how to stay visible to people looking for someone like you.*

Most job seekers start and end with one or two big job boards, but the openings that are easiest to find are also the ones with the most competition. Casting a slightly wider net — and knowing how to quickly judge whether a listing is worth your time — makes a meaningful difference in response rates. This guide covers where to look, how to evaluate what you find, and how to stay discoverable so opportunities also come to you.

## Company Career Pages

If you already have a list of companies you'd want to work for, their own career pages are often the best source, for a few reasons:

- Listings usually appear there before or at the same time as on aggregator boards, sometimes earlier.
- You're seeing the listing exactly as the company wrote it, without an aggregator's summary or reformatting.
- Some roles — especially at smaller companies — are only posted on the company site and never syndicated elsewhere.

A practical habit: keep a short list of 15-30 target companies and check their career pages on a regular rotation (weekly is usually enough) rather than relying on being alerted to new postings. Many companies also let you set up email alerts for new openings in a category — worth doing for your top handful of targets.

## Job Boards

General boards (large, broad-industry sites) are useful for volume and for surfacing roles you wouldn't have thought to search for, but they're also where the most applicants concentrate. A few ways to use them well:

- **Use specific search terms**, not just a job title — include tools, methodologies, or scope words that match how the role is likely described internally.
- **Filter by posting date** and prioritize recent listings; older listings may already have a full pipeline of candidates.
- **Set up saved searches or alerts** so new matches come to you instead of requiring a fresh manual search each time.

Niche and industry-specific boards are often lower-competition and higher-signal than general boards. If you're in a specific field — design, nonprofit work, healthcare, government, a particular tech stack — there is very likely a smaller board or community job list specific to it. These tend to attract fewer but more relevant applicants, and postings there often come from people who care enough about that niche to seek out the right channel.

## Referral Networks and Recruiters

Not every opening is publicly posted, and even for the ones that are, coming in through a person rather than a form changes how your application is treated.

- **Referral networks** — former colleagues, alumni groups, professional associations — are often the fastest route to an interview, because a referral gets a resume in front of a human instead of only an applicant tracking system. See the [Networking and Referrals](03-networking-referrals.md) guide for how to build and use this.
- **Recruiters** come in two flavors: in-house recruiters (employed by a single company, working only its open roles) and agency or third-party recruiters (working roles across multiple client companies, often specialized by industry or function). Agency recruiters can be a good source of leads you wouldn't find yourself, particularly in fields like finance, tech, and executive search — but vet them the same way you'd vet a job listing, since not every recruiter is representing a real, current opening.

## Evaluating a Listing

Before spending time tailoring an application, take five minutes to sanity-check the listing itself:

- **Is it current?** Check the posted date if available. A listing open for months may be a low-priority req, an evergreen posting, or one that's effectively already filled.
- **Does the seniority match?** A listing padded with an unrealistic number of "required" qualifications for the stated level can be a sign of a poorly scoped role — or just an aspirational wish list. Read the actual responsibilities, not just the requirements list, to judge fit.
- **Is the company real and the posting legitimate?** Confirm the company has a real presence (a working website, a findable presence on a professional network) and that the listing appears on their own site or a board you trust. Be cautious of listings that ask for sensitive personal or financial information before any interview has happened.
- **Does the scope match what you want?** Re-check the listing against the target you defined in [Job Search Strategy](01-job-search-strategy.md) — role, level, and non-negotiables — before deciding it's worth an application.

## Staying Discoverable

A meaningful share of opportunities come from someone finding you rather than the other way around — a recruiter searching LinkedIn, a hiring manager checking GitHub before a referral conversation, a former colleague thinking of you when a role opens on their team. Being findable and current costs little time and pays off unpredictably:

- Keep your LinkedIn headline and summary aligned with the roles you're actually targeting now, not a previous job's framing. Recruiters search by keyword, and a stale profile filters you out of searches you'd otherwise show up in.
- Turn on the visibility setting that lets recruiters know you're open, if that's appropriate given your current employment situation.
- If your field values it, keep GitHub (or a portfolio, or whatever the equivalent is for your discipline) reasonably current — a profile with recent, real activity reads very differently from one with a single project from three years ago.
- Small, occasional public activity — a comment, a share, a short post about something you learned — keeps you appearing in other people's feeds, a quieter but real form of staying discoverable.

None of this needs to be a major project. Twenty minutes of upkeep is usually enough to keep your public presence from working against you.
