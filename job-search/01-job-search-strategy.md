# Job Search Strategy

> **Format:** Markdown (`.md`) · **Designed for:** Humans + AI agents

*For anyone starting a search from scratch, or restarting one that's stalled — how to set a target, a pace, and a way to tell if it's working.*

A job search goes faster and feels less chaotic when you treat it like a project instead of a background task you squeeze in between other things. That means having a defined target, a schedule, and a way to know whether what you're doing is working. This guide covers how to set that foundation before you send a single application.

## Define Your Target

Vague goals like "I want a better job" make every other decision harder, because you can't evaluate a listing, tailor a resume, or know when to say yes to an offer without a reference point. Before you start applying, get specific about:

- **Role and level.** Are you searching for the same title you have now, a step up, or a lateral move into a new function? "Senior product manager" and "product manager, open to senior" are different searches with different competition.
- **Industry or company type.** Startup versus enterprise, in-person versus remote, mission-driven versus general commercial — these change what "a good fit" looks like and where you should be looking.
- **Compensation range.** Know your floor and your target before a recruiter asks, not while you're on the call.
- **Non-negotiables vs. nice-to-haves.** Separate "must be remote" from "would prefer remote." A list where everything is a dealbreaker will filter out roles you'd actually take.

Write this down somewhere you'll actually revisit — a single page is enough. It becomes the filter you run every listing through later.

## Timebox the Search

Job searches expand to fill the time you give them if you let them. Without structure, it's easy to spend a whole day "searching" and end up with two applications and a lot of scrolling. Instead:

- **Set a weekly cadence.** For example: two hours for finding and evaluating openings, three hours for tailoring and submitting applications, two hours for outreach and networking. Adjust the mix based on what stage you're in.
- **Give the search an end-of-week checkpoint.** Ask yourself: how many quality applications went out, how many conversations did I start, what moved forward? This isn't about hitting a number for its own sake — it's about noticing if a week produced nothing and changing something before another week goes by the same way.
- **Protect the time.** Treat search blocks like meetings you can't skip, especially if you're searching while employed. Searches drag on longest when they're the first thing to get bumped.
- **Set a review point, not just an end date.** Pick a date (e.g., three or six weeks in) to honestly assess whether your approach is producing responses. If it isn't, that's a signal to change targeting, materials, or channels — not to just keep going harder at the same thing.

## Track Your Effort, Not Just Outcomes

You don't need software to know whether your search is working — you need a habit of noticing. Keep a simple running note (a notebook, a doc, a spreadsheet, whatever you'll actually maintain) of:

- What you applied to and when
- Whether you customized materials for it or sent a general version
- Any response, and how long it took
- Any outreach or networking conversations connected to it

The point isn't precision, it's pattern recognition. If fifteen general applications produced zero responses but the three tailored ones produced two, that tells you where to put your energy next week. Effort without any record of it is very hard to learn from — you'll remember the rejections and forget the near-misses.

A simple spreadsheet is enough for this — company, role, date applied, status, and next action date, so nothing quietly dies just because you forgot to follow up on it. You don't need anything more elaborate than that to run a search well.

## Balance Volume vs. Targeting

There's a real tension between applying broadly (more shots on goal) and applying narrowly (higher quality per shot), and most people default too far in one direction.

- **Pure volume** — applying to everything remotely relevant with a generic resume — usually produces a low response rate, because hiring managers and applicant systems can tell when a resume wasn't written for the role.
- **Pure targeting** — spending hours perfecting one application — can stall a search entirely if you're only sending one or two a week.

A workable middle ground: maintain a shortlist of roles that are strong matches to your target (from the section above) and give those real tailoring time, while still applying to a wider set of reasonable-but-not-perfect matches with a solid, lightly adjusted version of your materials. Revisit the balance based on what your tracking shows — if targeted applications are converting to interviews and broad ones aren't, shift more time toward targeting.

## Know When to Adjust

Give any given approach two to three weeks of steady effort before judging it, not two or three days — hiring timelines are slow enough that short-term silence usually isn't a verdict. After that window, look at a few honest signals rather than your general mood about the search:

- **Response rate on well-matched applications.** If you're applying to genuinely good-fit roles and hearing nothing back at all, the problem is more likely the resume or the targeting than bad luck.
- **Where in the process you're losing momentum.** Getting first-round interviews but never advancing points to something specific — usually interview prep or a fit gap — which is a different fix than never getting a first interview at all.
- **Your own energy.** A search that's making you dread opening your email needs a change of pace or approach, not just more hours in the same direction.

When you do decide to adjust, change one variable at a time — the roles you're targeting, the resume, the channels you're using — instead of overhauling everything at once. That's the only way to actually learn what the problem was.
