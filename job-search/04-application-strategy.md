# Application Strategy

*For anyone deciding what to actually send, and to how many companies — the strategy behind a good application, not the structure of the resume itself.*

Once you've found openings worth pursuing, the next decision is how to actually apply to them — and specifically, how much time to spend per application versus how many to send. This is where most searches either stall out from over-perfecting a handful of applications, or burn effort on a flood of generic ones that never land. This guide covers how to prioritize and tailor applications so your time goes where it actually helps.

## Reading a Job Description for Signal

Most people skim a posting for the bullet points and miss most of what it's actually telling them. A posting carries more signal than its requirements list:

- **The order of responsibilities** usually reflects priority, not a random list — the first two or three bullets are typically what the role actually is day to day.
- **Repeated words or phrases** across the description point to what the team cares about most, and are worth genuinely using in your materials when they honestly apply to you.
- **The gap between "required" and "preferred"** tells you how strict the bar really is. A long "required" list for a role that's clearly hard to fill is sometimes softer in practice than it reads.
- **Who the role reports to, and how it's described,** hints at real scope — worth noticing before you invest tailoring time in a role that isn't what it appears to be.

A few extra minutes reading this way, before you touch your resume, tells you more than a few minutes spent tweaking formatting.

## Quality vs. Quantity

Neither extreme works well on its own:

- **All quantity** (sending the same resume everywhere) tends to produce a low response rate, because both applicant tracking systems and human reviewers can usually tell when a resume wasn't written with the role in mind.
- **All quality** (spending hours on every single application) can cap how many opportunities you're even in the running for, and not every listing deserves that level of investment.

A more sustainable approach is a tiered system based on how strong a match the role is to the target you defined in [Job Search Strategy](01-job-search-strategy.md):

- **Tier 1 — strong match, high priority.** Full tailoring: reworked resume bullets, a written cover letter, and ideally some networking or referral effort alongside the application (see [Networking and Referrals](03-networking-referrals.md)).
- **Tier 2 — decent match, worth a shot.** Light tailoring: adjust your resume summary and reorder or reword a few bullets to reflect the listing's language, skip a fully custom cover letter unless one is required.
- **Tier 3 — long shot or lower priority.** A well-maintained general version of your resume, submitted with minimal per-application time.

This lets you put concentrated effort where it's most likely to pay off, without letting the rest of the search go silent.

## What to Actually Customize

You don't need to rewrite a resume from scratch for each listing — a focused set of changes does most of the work:

- **Resume summary or headline.** This is the first thing read and the easiest place to mirror the language of the specific role.
- **Bullet order and emphasis.** Lead with the achievements most relevant to this listing; a bullet that's your third-most-important accomplishment for one role might be your best fit for another.
- **Keywords and terminology.** Match the specific tools, methods, or terms the listing uses, as long as they honestly reflect your experience — this matters both for a human skimming quickly and for applicant tracking systems that filter on keyword matches.
- **Cover letter, when you write one.** Reference the company and role specifically, and connect one or two of your concrete achievements to what the listing says they need. A cover letter that could be sent to any company for any role signals low effort, even if the writing itself is fine.

What you generally shouldn't change per application: your core work history, dates, and factual accomplishments. Tailoring is about emphasis and framing, not rewriting your actual experience to fit each listing.

## Matching Your Materials to the Listing

Before submitting, read the listing one more time next to your resume and ask:

- Does my summary reflect the actual title and level of this role, not just my current title?
- Do my top 2-3 bullets speak directly to the top 2-3 responsibilities in the listing?
- If the listing lists specific required skills, are the ones I genuinely have visible and easy to find on the resume?
- Would a reviewer skimming for 30 seconds see the connection between my background and this specific role?

If the answer to that last question is no, it's usually worth one more editing pass rather than submitting as-is — a resume that requires the reader to connect the dots themselves is easy to pass over.

## Pacing Your Applications

Tie this back to the cadence from [Job Search Strategy](01-job-search-strategy.md): decide roughly how many Tier 1, Tier 2, and Tier 3 applications you're aiming for each week, rather than treating every application as equally worth full effort. A workable rough split for many searches is a small number of heavily tailored Tier 1 applications alongside a larger number of lightly tailored Tier 2 and 3 ones — adjust the ratio based on what your own tracking shows is converting to responses.
